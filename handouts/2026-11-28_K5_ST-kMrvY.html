Stop Paying LLM Prices for Yes-or-No Answers — Chinmay Goyal, Glean

AI Engineer · 16 min talk · Watch · Transcript · 2026-11-28 10:38 UTC · gemini-4-hydrogen

AI products are full of bounded decisions whose outputs can be listed in advance, and a hosted zero-shot decision model picks them faster, cheaper, and more repeatably than a generative LLM.

TL;DR

  • Most AI product steps are bounded decisions - routes, ranks, verdicts - yet teams pay full autoregressive LLM latency and cost to pick them.
  • Zero-shot Jev reached 90% of fine-tuned classification agreement with no training; label changes need a prompt edit, not a retrain.
  • Production swaps paid off: 8.1x median speedup on expert-routed calls; ranker p50 down 4.67 s to 0.67 s; timeouts 10.2% to 0.8%.
  • Guardrail first-pass recall tripled at about the same precision, retiring per-customer T4 GPU classifier fleets.

Chinmay Goyal, software engineer on Glean Assistant, presented Glean's internal results at AI Engineer Code Summit. Glean evaluated Jev, a hosted zero-shot decision API returning label probabilities, against incumbent LLM and classifier baselines on five tasks: query classification, expert routing, suggestion ranking, citation judging, and guardrail first-pass checks. All figures are Glean self-reported from test beds, golden sets, and internal deployment; baselines, windows, and N differ per task.

Deep dives

[02:20] Staged evaluation before users see a change

  • Golden eval sets exist for every surface with any intelligence; changes replay there first.
  • Disagreements are hand-labeled: 59 Jev vs OpenAI routing splits, to expose failure patterns.
  • Arms ramped 10% to 50% of internal chat sessions; roughly 1,500 Glean users soak changes for weeks with active feedback, then customer A/B.

Why it matters: A transferable gate order for model swaps: offline replay, hand-labeled splits, armed internal rollout, then customer experiment. (inference)

Watch out: Internal users at one company may not match customer traffic or taxonomy mix.

[08:55] Probabilities turn a decision into tunable policy

  • The decision model returns a probability per option, not a binary answer.
  • Abstention is a first-class outcome, logged separately from failures; cutoffs are per-surface config flags, 0.40 default in the production experiment.
  • Self-learning systems tune thresholds per customer deployment; routing transfers only on a confident pick.

Why it matters: Confidence outputs let one model hold different operating points per surface and customer without retraining. (inference)

Watch out: Per-surface, per-customer cutoffs multiply configuration to own, audit, and debug.

[12:58] Guardrails: filter first, arbitrate second

  • Prompts, tool outputs, and responses pass a fast parallel first-pass check; an LLM makes the final call on flags.
  • The old first pass was a virtual classifier on T4 GPUs per customer - single-tenant, with an assumed 1,000 customers.
  • Two to three engineers maintained those fleets full time; the swap removed that burden.

Why it matters: A cheap high-recall screen feeding an expensive arbiter is the cost shape; per-tenant classifier fleets are an ops tax an API removes. (inference)

Watch out: The first pass is not standalone safety; the final LLM still arbitrates every flag.

[14:44] Screening rule: generate, decide, or hybridize

  • Unlistable output? Keep a generative model. Listable? Benchmark Jev.
  • Stable labels plus high volume: also benchmark a fine-tuned classifier.
  • Dynamic text needed? Hybrid - Jev picks the tool, the LLM writes the arguments.

Why it matters: Enumerating each step's candidate outputs becomes the scoping conversation itself - the cheapest generate-versus-decide test. (inference)

Watch out: Jev produces no generated text; explanations and freeform arguments still need an LLM.

Field playbook (inferred from the talk, not claims made in it)

Ask the customer

  • Which pipeline steps pick from a fixed set - routing, ranking, gating, verdicts - and what does each call cost?
  • How often do your labels or taxonomy change, and who pays to retrain?
  • What latency budget does each decision step have, and what does a timeout cost the product?
  • Do you host per-customer classifiers today, and how many engineers maintain them?

Measure in a pilot

  • Step latency p50 and p95, old versus new, against the step's stated time budget.
  • Golden-set agreement with the incumbent, plus verdict flips across identical reruns.
  • Timeouts, invalid outputs, and abstentions at the chosen cutoff, logged separately.

Where it breaks

  • The decision call blocks the no-action path, slowing most requests.
  • Cutoffs tuned per surface or customer drift apart, starving some surfaces or flooding others.
  • Prompt-edited label sets silently shift behavior without golden-set re-baselining.

What the talk does not establish

  • No end-user or business outcomes; quality evidence is step-level agreement, precision, and judge consistency.
  • Jev itself is uncharacterized - no training data, calibration method, serving stack, or pricing described.
  • All results are Glean self-reported on internal workloads; no external or cross-customer replication.
  • The prompt-edit-replaces-retrain claim for changing taxonomies is asserted, never measured.

Checklist

  • List every decision step in the target pipeline; mark which outputs can be enumerated in advance. [14:44]
  • Replay a golden set through incumbent and decision model before any live traffic. [02:34]
  • Hand-label a sample of old-versus-new disagreements to find failure patterns. [02:20]
  • Run three identical passes on eval inputs and count verdict flips before trusting a judge. [11:02]
  • Set a per-surface confidence cutoff and log abstentions separately from failures. [08:52]
  • Benchmark the no-transfer path and budget integration work before promising routing speedups. [05:19]
